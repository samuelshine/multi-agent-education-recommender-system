\begin{abstract}
Students choosing a university and program must weigh academic fit, career goals,
financial constraints, location, and employability, usually with no prior interaction
history to learn from. Classical educational recommenders address this pre-admission,
cross-institution decision through collaborative, content-based, or knowledge-based
filtering but remain static and weakly explainable. Recent agentic, LLM-powered
recommenders add adaptivity, planning, memory, and explainability, but published
2025--2026 systems target already-enrolled students in an already-chosen program, not
the pre-admission choice. We propose a five-agent architecture (profiling, retrieval,
multi-objective recommendation, explanation, and memory-reflection) for this gap,
evaluated against re-implemented classical baselines (HRSPCA, StudieMe) and a
single-prompt LLM ablation on a released synthetic benchmark. The architecture does not
win on oracle-graded top-$k$ accuracy, but wins decisively on explanation quality:
91--100\% of explanations are grounded in retrieved evidence against 36--67\% for the
single-prompt baseline. Memory-and-reflection improves session-over-session quality.
Explainability, not accuracy, is the architecture's demonstrated contribution; the
prototype and dataset are open-sourced.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Choosing a university and program is a high-stakes, low-frequency decision made under
incomplete information. Prospective students must reconcile academic interests, career
goals, skills, financial constraints, location preferences, and employability outcomes,
usually without any prior interaction history a recommender system could exploit: the
canonical cold-start setting. A substantial body of work addresses this decision with
collaborative, content-based, knowledge-based, and hybrid or machine-learning
classification approaches \cite{hrspca,studieme,multidmensionalrecsysuni,companalysisrecsys,
universityrecsyselahi,bertopicprogramrec}. These systems established strong evaluation
methodology and real datasets, but a 2024 systematic review documents recurring
limitations: static pipelines, minimal conversational interaction, and weak explainability
\cite{recommendersyshighered}.

Separately, LLM-powered agents have produced a new recommendation paradigm: systems that
plan, retrieve, reflect, and maintain long-term memory rather than scoring a fixed candidate
set once \cite{llmagentrecsyssurvey}. Agentic recommenders of this kind have been
demonstrated in e-commerce and general content domains
\cite{agenticaiframeworkrecommendersystem,taurecbenchmark,ragpersonalisedrecommendations},
and, more recently, in education, but specifically for
students who are \emph{already enrolled} in an \emph{already-chosen} program: advising
Q\&A \cite{advisingwise}, learning-pathway sequencing \cite{agenticedurec}, and course
selection \cite{onlinecoursemaskg}. None of these systems address the earlier, arguably
higher-stakes decision of \emph{which} institution and program to choose.

This paper's central claim is that this earlier decision (pre-admission, cross-institution
selection) is a distinct decision scope from the in-program advising/pathway/course-selection
problems 2025--2026 agentic education systems solve, and remains addressed only by
pre-agentic methods.

Contributions: (i) an agentic multi-agent architecture targeting pre-admission,
cross-institution decision support, distinct from in-program agentic-education work
(Section~\ref{sec:related}); (ii) an explicit multi-objective reasoning mechanism
evaluated against classical baselines on both standard IR metrics and agentic-specific
metrics (explanation quality, adaptivity, cold-start robustness) that recent surveys call
for but no education-specific evaluation yet supplies \cite{llmagentrecsyssurvey}; (iii) a
working prototype, evaluation harness, and results, released as an open-source artifact:
\url{https://github.com/samuelshine/multi-agent-education-recommender-system}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

HRSPCA \cite{hrspca} and StudieMe \cite{studieme} target the same pre-admission,
cross-institution scope as this paper, via hybrid collaborative filtering with
knowledge-based rules \cite{hrspca} or content-based TF-IDF similarity \cite{studieme}.
Somy\"urek and Aksoy \cite{multidmensionalrecsysuni} extend this to joint
university-and-major selection, the closest classical match to the decision scope
adopted here. Elahi et al.\ \cite{universityrecsyselahi} evaluate a collaborative-filtering
university recommender via offline cross-validation plus an online user study, a protocol
this paper adopts as a template (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). El-Keiey et al.\
\cite{careerrecfeatureselection} and Hill et al.\ \cite{bertopicprogramrec} (both 2025)
address adjacent career/program recommendation with single-model, non-agentic pipelines.
None reason via autonomous agents, maintain cross-session memory, or generate grounded
explanations, confirmed as recurring limitations by a systematic review
\cite{recommendersyshighered}.

In the general-domain agentic literature, Portugal et al.\
\cite{agenticaiframeworkrecommendersystem} propose a multi-agent recommender framework
whose title closely resembles this paper's title (the closest general-domain
precedent) but target e-commerce with no educational features or metrics. Narasimhan
and Narasimhan \cite{taurecbenchmark} introduce a general-domain agentic benchmark; no
education-specific one yet exists. Peng et al.'s survey \cite{llmagentrecsyssurvey} covers
e-commerce, media, and conversation; higher education appears in neither its
applications nor its future directions.

Multi-agent educational recommenders predate LLMs; the novelty here rests on combining
agentic LLM capabilities (planning, memory, reflection, grounded explanation) with the
pre-admission scope, not on multi-agent structure alone. Three 2025--2026 systems combine
multi-agent LLM methods with an educational target. AdvisingWise \cite{advisingwise} is a human-in-the-loop system
answering advising questions for \emph{already-enrolled} students, with every response
human-validated; it does not recommend a program. Chaturvedi and Gunawardena
\cite{agenticedurec} deploy an 18-agent hierarchy for in-program pathway recommendation at
production scale, addressing only post-enrollment adaptation. Zhong et al.\
\cite{onlinecoursemaskg} propose a three-agent architecture (profiling, retrieval,
generation) with a course knowledge graph (structurally the closest precedent to
Section~\ref{sec:architecture}), but scoped to in-curriculum course choice, without
multi-objective reasoning over career, financial, location, and employability constraints.
Table~\ref{tab:gap} summarizes the resulting gap.

\section{Proposed Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

An initial seven-agent brainstorm (profiling, goal planning, retrieval, recommendation,
reflection, explanation, memory) was merged to five: Zhong et al.\
\cite{onlinecoursemaskg} report gains with only three agents, while Chaturvedi and
Gunawardena's 18+ agents \cite{agenticedurec} require production-scale engineering not
reproducible in a small-team research prototype. Explanation and memory-reflection remain
separate, visible components since they map directly to this paper's headline claims;
goal-planning's constraint logic folds into profiling and recommendation.

\textbf{Profiling \& Constraint Agent} extracts a structured student-constraint object
single-turn per session; continuity comes from the Memory \& Reflection Agent's persisted
state (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, dashed arrow). \textbf{Retrieval Agent} selects
programs worth presenting from a deterministic hard-constraint prefilter's output
(admission, budget, location); this is LLM-based relevance selection over a prefiltered
candidate list, not embedding retrieval, following \cite{ragpersonalisedrecommendations}.
\textbf{Recommendation \& Planning Agent} ranks candidates under multiple, potentially
conflicting objectives: the mechanism RQ2 evaluates. \textbf{Memory \& Reflection Agent}
reviews the shortlist for constraint violations and persists state across sessions: the
mechanism RQ3 evaluates; a second, deterministic guard repeats the hard-constraint check
after it. \textbf{Explanation Agent} generates a per-recommendation rationale grounded in
the Retrieval Agent's evidence, addressing the explainability limitation
\cite{recommendersyshighered} documents in classical systems. Relative to the closest
agentic precedent's profiling, retrieval, and generation agents \cite{onlinecoursemaskg},
the new elements are constraint elicitation over financial, location, and employability
objectives (Profiling), explicit trade-off ranking across them (Recommendation), a
reflection pass with cross-session memory (Memory \& Reflection), and an explanation
step restricted to retrieved evidence (Explanation). The two deterministic
prefilter/guard components, not agent judgment alone, actually guarantee hard-constraint
satisfaction. The 7$\to$5 merge is a buildability judgment, not an ablation result; H3's
memory-and-reflection results (Section~\ref{sec:results}, evaluated as a single fused
mechanism) provide the retrospective check and motivate no split back apart, though a
finer-grained ablation isolating the two is left to future work.

\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:methodology}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.6em, labelwidth=2.3em, labelsep=0.3em, align=left, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{RQ1.}] Does the architecture improve explainability and adaptivity over
classical recommenders \cite{hrspca,studieme,multidmensionalrecsysuni} within a 5\%
non-inferiority margin of their accuracy (precision@$k$, recall@$k$, NDCG)?
\item[\textbf{RQ2.}] How should multi-objective constraints be represented and reasoned
over by specialized agents, compared to a single monolithic LLM prompt or classical
hybrid scoring?
\item[\textbf{RQ3.}] Do the Memory \& Reflection Agent's mechanisms measurably reduce
cold-start degradation compared to a stateless pipeline?
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.9em, labelwidth=1.6em, labelsep=0.3em, align=left, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{H1.}] The architecture achieves higher explanation quality than a
classical hybrid baseline re-implemented from \cite{hrspca,studieme}, within a
pre-registered\footnote{Metric choice, $k=5$, and the 5\% margin were committed to the
public repository (commit \texttt{9b47ce4}, 2026-07-17) before any agentic-condition
result was computed; results were written up in a later commit (\texttt{ba7757e},
2026-07-20).} 5\% relative non-inferiority margin on NDCG@$k$.
\item[\textbf{H2.}] Explicit multi-objective reasoning improves relevance for students
with conflicting constraints relative to a single-objective baseline.
\item[\textbf{H3.}] Memory \& Reflection mitigates cold-start degradation more than a
stateless pipeline, across simulated first-vs.-later-session interactions.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Datasets.} The intended primary H1 benchmark is Elahi et al.'s
\cite{universityrecsyselahi} university-rating dataset (1{,}515 ratings, 80 users, 551
universities), available on request; that request was still pending when experiments ran,
so results here use a released synthetic constraint-preference benchmark instead (a
self-constructed-benchmark limitation, Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), which also carries
the H2/H3 conditions rating-only data cannot express. It
regenerates deterministically from 300 synthetic students sampled from the public
20{,}000-record IT-student corpus of \cite{careerrecfeatureselection} (financial,
location, and employability constraints attached by documented rules), a fictional
200-program catalog, and graded relevance (0--3) from a published oracle utility function
over academic fit, financial feasibility, location, and employability, fixed before
evaluation. Each student carries a three-session progressive-disclosure script;
162/300 students have a financially infeasible best-fitting program (the H2 condition).

\textbf{Baselines.} (a) a re-implementation of HRSPCA's cascaded rule/predictor pipeline
\cite{hrspca}; (b) a re-implementation of StudieMe's content-based ranker \cite{studieme};
(c) a single-prompt LLM baseline performing profiling, ranking, and explanation in one
call, following \cite{onlinecoursemaskg}. Both classical baselines were re-implemented
from the original papers' full texts, with every underspecified detail resolved by a
logged best-faith choice (13 choices, released in the artifact); two original components
could not be expressed in the evaluation catalog, favoring the baselines
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). All agentic conditions run on a local open-weights Gemma
model via Ollama (\texttt{gemma4:e4b}), provider-agnostic in the released artifact.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The headline metric is NDCG@5 with graded relevance (0--3), chosen for rank-sensitivity and
because it uses the oracle's graded relevance directly rather than a binary cutoff; $k=5$
matches both the baselines' reconstructed output length and a plausible shortlist size.
Precision@5 and recall@5 are secondary; all systems are evaluated on the same held-out
split of 100 students, under the pre-registered 5\% non-inferiority margin (H1).

Explanation quality is scored by LLM-as-judge on a three-dimension rubric (clarity,
groundedness, usefulness; 1--5 each), following \cite{advisingwise}. To avoid
self-preference bias, the judge is a different model family than the experiment model
(primary: Qwen-family 14B-class; a Mistral-family second judge gives inter-judge
agreement), with system identity blinded and absolute scoring.

Conditions: (i) the full five-agent pipeline; (ii) a no-memory ablation (H3); (iii) a
single-objective ablation (H2); (iv) the single-prompt baseline; (v) the two classical
baselines. H1 uses the full-information state; H3 uses the three-session
progressive-disclosure structure, with simulated-user feedback derived from the oracle's
utility ranking, identical across every system; this is discussed as a limitation in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\textbf{The pre-registered accuracy criterion is not met.} Five-agent v1 reaches NDCG@5
$=0.706$, an 11.0\% relative shortfall against HRSPCA, and the single-prompt baseline
($0.830$) outperforms both. \textbf{Diagnostics} attribute this to shortlist
\emph{abstention}, not ranking quality: Reflection trims candidates rather than padding
with constraint-violating ones, so 70\% of shortlists contain fewer than five items (mean
3.9), and NDCG@5 penalizes every unfilled position. Measured at emitted length, v1 scores
$0.827$ against $0.830$ (paired bootstrap, $n=100$: mean diff $-0.005$, 95\% CI
$[-0.024,+0.014]$, not significant), and precision@5 matches. A post-registration revision
(v2, reported alongside not in place of v1) restores list length to 4.8 by dropping only
for hard-constraint violations; it reaches NDCG@5 $=0.806$, clearing the pre-registered bar
and slightly exceeding HRSPCA, confirming the abstention diagnosis, but remains 2.9\%
relative below the single-prompt baseline, so neither pruning policy shows a top-$k$
accuracy advantage over a single LLM call.

\subsection{Multi-objective conflict (H2)}

On the 56 conflict-subset students, v1's multi-objective pipeline scores NDCG@5 $=0.666$
against $0.718$ single-objective, again driven by shorter conflict-case shortlists (mean
3.70 vs.\ 4.02). Under v2, multi-objective reaches $0.792$ (mean 4.77 items) against
$0.793$ single-objective (paired bootstrap, $n=56$: mean diff $-0.001$, 95\% CI
$[-0.017,+0.014]$, not significant). \textbf{H2 is not supported on oracle-graded
accuracy}: the oracle's utility already encodes all four objectives, so a
single-objective ranker over a constraint-prefiltered pool recovers most of the signal;
whether multi-objective reasoning earns its place in \emph{explanations} is assessed below.

\subsection{Cold start and memory (H3)}

On a fixed 30-student progressive-disclosure cohort, full and no-memory conditions are
identical at session 1 by construction (both NDCG@5 $=0.583$). With memory, session-2
accuracy reaches $0.776$ vs.\ $0.735$ without ($+0.041$), and the gap persists at session 3
($0.748$ vs.\ $0.729$); recall@5 degrades session-2-to-3 without memory ($0.126\to0.090$)
but holds with it ($0.118\to0.115$). The effect is directionally consistent but modest at
$n=30$; progressive disclosure itself is the largest driver of improvement in both
conditions. H3 is supported in direction, with the caveat that ``cold-start mitigation'' is
more precisely an \emph{adaptivity} gain: the first session is unaffected by construction.

\textbf{Isolating architecture from instruction.} The single-prompt baseline's explanation
instruction is weaker than \textsc{Explain}'s, confounding architecture with wording. A
fourth condition, single-prompt (grounded), upgrades only the instruction to
\textsc{Explain}'s directive verbatim (Table~\ref{tab:results-h1}); it scores clarity
$3.81$, groundedness $4.47$, usefulness $3.23$ (between the ungrounded baseline and the
five-agent conditions), and its NDCG@5/precision@5 match or slightly exceed the
ungrounded baseline's, so the instruction change costs nothing on ranking. Decomposing the
ungrounded-to-v1 gap (paired bootstrap, $n=100$): the instruction effect accounts for 53\%
of the groundedness gap ($+0.515$, 95\% CI $[+0.320,+0.705]$), 50\% of usefulness
($+0.425$, 95\% CI $[+0.295,+0.545]$), and 29\% of clarity ($+0.120$, 95\% CI
$[+0.035,+0.205]$); the residual architecture effect accounts for the rest, and none of
the six intervals crosses zero. Both grounding instructions and architectural
decomposition make a real, statistically supported contribution, with neither alone
accounting for it.

This is corroborated by an invented-claims failure signal: the single-prompt baseline was
flagged in 64/100 explanations by the primary judge (33/100 by the agreement judge)
against 5--9/100 for the five-agent conditions; the grounding instruction alone cuts the
baseline's rate to 42/100 and 6/100. Inter-judge agreement is correspondingly tighter for
the five-agent conditions (mean $|$diff$|$ 0.09--0.15) than the single-prompt family
(1.26). Restricting this analysis to the 56 H2 conflict students reproduces the gap
unchanged: multi-objective reasoning does not win on oracle-graded ranking, but produces
more reliably grounded explanations for students facing a real trade-off.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The gap argument in Section~\ref{sec:related} was verified against full-text readings of
the decisive prior work: all three agentic-education systems
\cite{advisingwise,agenticedurec,onlinecoursemaskg} address only already-enrolled students,
and both re-implemented classical baselines \cite{hrspca,studieme} contain no agentic,
dialogue, or memory elements (peripheral citations remain verified at the abstract level
only). The 7-to-5 agent merge is a buildability judgment, not an empirical result.

\textbf{Oracle circularity and synthetic data.} Ground-truth relevance is a published
utility function fixed before evaluation, not human judgment; a system that
reverse-engineered it would score perfectly. The oracle is documented in the released
dataset card and identical for all systems, and explanation quality is judged
independently of it. The catalog is fictional, constraints follow documented sampling
rules, and the three-session structure is scripted with oracle-derived simulated feedback.
Career coverage is IT-only, inherited from \cite{careerrecfeatureselection};
generalization beyond IT-bound students is untested.

\textbf{Baseline reconstruction fidelity.} Both baselines were re-implemented from their
papers' full texts, with underspecified details resolved by logged best-faith choices
(released in the artifact). Two original components (HRSPCA's college-capacity rule and
StudieMe's faculty-matching stage) could not be expressed in the catalog; both omissions
favor the baselines, not the proposed system.

\textbf{LLM-as-judge validity and model scale.} Explanation scores come from LLM judges,
not human raters; cross-family judging, blinding, and two-judge agreement reduce but do
not eliminate known judge biases, and this study performs no human-subject validation.
Retrieval, reflection, and the explanation agent were not ablated separately, so their
individual contributions to the groundedness gain are not isolated. All
agentic conditions run on one local model (\texttt{gemma4:e4b}), so conclusions are
conditioned on this model class; the evaluation split ($n=100$) is larger than the closest
classical baselines' user studies but small in absolute terms.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

For pre-admission program selection, agentic decomposition improves explanation
quality, not ranking accuracy. Three conclusions follow from the evidence. First, accuracy
and explainability are separable: the five-agent pipeline matches or exceeds the classical
state of the art (NDCG@5 $0.806$ vs.\ $0.793$ for HRSPCA) but not a single LLM call
($0.830$), while grounding 91--100\% of its explanations in retrieved evidence against
36--67\% for that call, with far fewer invented claims. Second, about half of this
groundedness gain comes from instruction wording and half from the architecture's
dedicated explanation step over agent-curated evidence; both effects are statistically
supported. Designers who cannot deploy a multi-agent pipeline can therefore get part of
the benefit from a grounding instruction alone. Third, multi-objective ranking adds no
oracle-graded accuracy when hard constraints are already prefiltered, and cross-session
memory gives a modest adaptivity gain ($+0.041$ NDCG@5 at session 2), not a cold-start
fix. Agentic architectures for admission advising should thus be justified by
trustworthy, auditable explanations rather than by ranking gains, at least at this
model scale. These conclusions rest on synthetic data and LLM judges; the next steps are
a human study with prospective students and academic advisors, evaluation on real
preference data such as Elahi et al.'s \cite{universityrecsyselahi}, and a
component-level ablation separating retrieval, reflection, memory persistence, and the
explanation agent. The prototype, evaluation harness, and synthetic dataset are released
as an open-source artifact so these directions do not depend on the authors'
environment.
