\begin{abstract}
Students choosing which university and program to apply to must jointly reason over
academic fit, career goals, financial constraints, location preferences, and employability
outcomes, typically before they have generated any interaction history a recommender can
learn from. Classical educational recommender systems address this pre-admission,
cross-institution decision using collaborative, content-based, or knowledge-based filtering,
but remain static, weakly explainable, and brittle under cold start. Recent agentic,
LLM-powered multi-agent recommenders address adaptivity, planning, memory, and
explainability, but published 2025--2026 systems apply these capabilities to
\emph{already-enrolled} students inside an \emph{already-chosen} program rather than to
the pre-admission choice itself. We propose a five-agent architecture (profiling and
constraint elicitation, retrieval, multi-objective recommendation and planning,
explanation, and memory-reflection) for this gap and evaluate a prototype against
faithfully re-implemented classical baselines (HRSPCA, StudieMe) and a single-prompt LLM
ablation, on a released synthetic constraint-preference benchmark. The architecture does
not win on oracle-graded top-$k$ accuracy: it falls outside a pre-registered 5\%
non-inferiority margin under its initial design, and a diagnosed-and-corrected variant
exceeds both classical baselines but not the single-prompt ablation. It wins decisively,
however, on explanation quality as scored by two independent LLM judges -- grounded in
retrieved evidence in 91--100\% of explanations against 36--67\% for the single-prompt
baseline, an advantage only half attributable to prompt wording (an ablation adding the
same grounding instruction to the single-prompt baseline closes roughly half the gap,
isolating a residual architectural effect) -- and on multi-objective conflict cases,
where the same groundedness advantage holds even though multi-objective ranking does not
outperform a single-objective ranker on oracle-graded relevance. A memory-and-reflection mechanism modestly improves
session-over-session recommendation quality relative to a stateless pipeline. These
results support explainability, not accuracy, as the architecture's demonstrated
contribution over both classical and single-prompt alternatives, and the full prototype,
evaluation harness, and synthetic dataset are released as an open-source artifact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Choosing a university and program is a high-stakes, low-frequency decision made under
incomplete information. Prospective students must reconcile academic interests, career
goals, skills, financial constraints, location preferences, and employability outcomes,
usually without any prior interaction history a recommender system could exploit -- the
canonical cold-start setting. A substantial body of work addresses this decision with
collaborative filtering, content-based filtering, knowledge-based recommendation, and
hybrid or machine-learning classification approaches
\cite{hrspca,studieme,universityadmissionrecommendersystem,smartcollegeadmrecommendationsys,
userprofilinguniversityrecsys,contentbasedrecsys,multidmensionalrecsysuni,companalysisrecsys,
universityrecsyselahi,bertopicprogramrec}.
These systems established strong evaluation methodology, real datasets, and
education-specific features, but a 2024 systematic review of the space documents recurring
limitations: static recommendation pipelines, limited personalization, minimal conversational
interaction, weak adaptation over time, and weak explainability
\cite{recommendersyshighered}.

Separately, the emergence of large language model (LLM)-powered agents has produced a new
paradigm for recommendation: systems that plan, retrieve, reflect, and maintain long-term
memory across a conversation rather than scoring a fixed candidate set once
\cite{llmagentrecsyssurvey,agenticaisurvey,agenticaiarchitectures}. Agentic recommenders of
this kind have been demonstrated in e-commerce and general content domains
\cite{agenticaiframeworkrecommendersystem,multiagentrecommendersys,taurecbenchmark,
ragpersonalisedrecommendations}, and, more recently, in education -- but specifically for
students who are \emph{already enrolled} in an \emph{already-chosen} program: academic
advising Q\&A \cite{advisingwise}, in-program learning-pathway sequencing
\cite{agenticedurec}, and in-curriculum course selection \cite{onlinecoursemaskg}. None of
these systems address the earlier, arguably higher-stakes decision of \emph{which}
institution and program to choose in the first place.

This paper's central claim is that this earlier decision -- pre-admission, cross-institution
university and program selection -- is a distinct decision scope from the in-program
advising/pathway/course-selection problems that 2025--2026 agentic education systems solve,
and that it remains addressed only by pre-agentic methods. Section~\ref{sec:related}
substantiates this claim against the closest prior work in both the classical educational
recommender literature and the agentic recommender literature. Section~\ref{sec:architecture}
proposes a five-agent architecture for this gap. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} and
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} lay out the research questions, hypotheses, and evaluation
protocol; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the resulting experiments on a working
prototype.

This paper's contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An agentic multi-agent LLM architecture targeting pre-admission, cross-institution
higher-education decision support -- a decision scope distinct from the in-program
advising/pathway/course systems in recent agentic-education work (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
\item An explicit multi-objective reasoning mechanism integrating career goals, financial
constraints, location, and employability into agent planning, evaluated against
classical educational-RS baselines on both standard information-retrieval metrics and
agentic-specific metrics (explanation quality, adaptivity, cold-start robustness) --
metrics that recent agentic-recommendation surveys call for more generally
\cite{llmagentrecsyssurvey} but that no education-specific evaluation yet supplies.
\item A working prototype, evaluation harness, and evaluation results
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), released as an open-source artifact that third parties can
run and independently verify: \url{https://github.com/samuelshine/multi-agent-education-recommender-system}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Classical educational recommenders for pre-admission decisions}

HRSPCA \cite{hrspca} and StudieMe \cite{studieme} are representative of the pre-admission,
cross-institution decision scope this paper targets: both recommend which
college/university a prospective student should consider, using hybrid collaborative
filtering with knowledge-based rules \cite{hrspca} or content-based TF-IDF similarity
\cite{studieme}. Somy\"urek and Aksoy \cite{multidmensionalrecsysuni} extend this to joint
university-and-major selection with a multidimensional recommender, the closest classical
match to the decision scope adopted here. Elahi et al.\ \cite{universityrecsyselahi}
develop a collaborative-filtering university recommender evaluated in two phases -- offline
cross-validation followed by an online user study measuring accuracy, diversity, perceived
personalization, satisfaction, and novelty -- an evaluation protocol this paper adopts as a
template (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). Lahoud et al.\ \cite{companalysisrecsys} compare
several such systems for major/career guidance and report fragmentation across methods
rather than a unified adaptive framework; El-Keiey et al.\ \cite{careerrecfeatureselection}
address the adjacent career-path decision with feature-selected machine-learning
classification over a public 20{,}000-record student-features dataset, and Hill et
al.\ \cite{bertopicprogramrec} recommend academic programs to prospective students via
BERTopic interest mining -- both from 2025, and both still single-model, non-agentic
pipelines. None of these systems reason via autonomous
planning agents, maintain memory across sessions, or generate grounded natural-language
explanations; Kamal et al.'s systematic review \cite{recommendersyshighered} confirms these
as recurring, unresolved limitations across the sub-field. By contrast,
\cite{serendipityuniversitycourserecsys} and \cite{recommendationsyscomplexconstraints}
address a related but distinct \emph{post-enrollment} scope (in-program course selection),
which this paper does not target.

\subsection{Agentic and multi-agent recommenders (general domain)}

Portugal et al.\ \cite{agenticaiframeworkrecommendersystem} propose an agentic multi-agent
recommender framework whose title closely resembles this paper's working title, making it
the closest general-domain precedent; it targets e-commerce recommendation and does not
incorporate educational features, datasets, or evaluation metrics. Yousefi Maragheh et
al.\ \cite{multiagentrecommendersys} survey multi-agentic design patterns for industrial
recommendation, and Narasimhan and Narasimhan \cite{taurecbenchmark} introduce a verifiable
benchmark for agentic recommenders, evaluated on general-domain conversational tasks across
five model families -- no education-specific agentic-recommendation benchmark yet exists.
Peng et al.'s survey \cite{llmagentrecsyssurvey} categorizes agentic recommendation into
recommender-oriented, interaction-oriented, and simulation-oriented paradigms across
e-commerce, media, and conversational domains; higher education appears in neither its
surveyed applications nor its three named future directions (architecture optimization,
evaluation-framework standardization, adversarial security), underscoring how unexplored
the domain remains even in a survey dedicated to this exact method family -- the gap this
paper addresses directly.

\subsection{Agentic multi-agent systems in education}

Multi-agent educational recommenders predate LLMs: Marivate et al.\
\cite{humancapacitybuildingmas} deployed cooperative collaborative-filtering agents for
professional course selection in 2008, and Viswanathan \cite{adaptivemaselearning} surveys
the pre-LLM adaptive multi-agent e-learning line. The novelty claimed here therefore rests
on the combination of \emph{agentic LLM} capabilities (planning, memory, reflection,
grounded explanation) with the pre-admission decision scope -- not on multi-agent structure
alone. Three 2025--2026 systems are the decisive comparison set, since each combines
multi-agent LLM methods with an educational target. AdvisingWise \cite{advisingwise} is a
human-in-the-loop multi-agent system that answers academic-advising questions for
\emph{already-enrolled} graduate students within a fixed program, with all responses
validated by a human advisor before delivery -- it does not recommend which program to
choose, and its human-in-loop response drafting differs from autonomous multi-agent
planning. Chaturvedi and Gunawardena \cite{agenticedurec} deploy an 18-agent, four-tier
hierarchical architecture for adaptive in-program learning-pathway recommendation at
production scale (6{,}000+ users), but likewise address only \emph{post-enrollment}
curriculum adaptation. Zhong et al.\ \cite{onlinecoursemaskg} propose a three-agent
architecture (learner profiling, knowledge retrieval, recommendation generation) coupled
with a course knowledge graph for \emph{in-curriculum} course recommendation --
structurally the closest precedent to the architecture proposed in
Section~\ref{sec:architecture}, but scoped to course-level choice for enrolled students
rather than institution/program-level choice for prospective students, and without
multi-objective reasoning over career, financial, location, and employability constraints.

Table~\ref{tab:gap} summarizes the resulting gap: no system in the surveyed literature
combines an agentic multi-agent architecture with the pre-admission, cross-institution
decision scope while reasoning jointly over the constraint set a prospective student
actually faces.

\section{Proposed Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The initial brainstorm for this project proposed seven agents (profiling, goal planning,
knowledge retrieval, recommendation, reflection, explanation, memory). Two of the closest
comparison systems bound a reasonable agent count: Zhong et al.\ \cite{onlinecoursemaskg}
report accuracy, recall, and F1 gains with only three agents, while Chaturvedi and
Gunawardena \cite{agenticedurec} use 18+ agents but only at production deployment scale
with a dedicated engineering team -- not reproducible within a single-author prototype.
This paper accordingly merges the original seven agents into five, keeping explanation and
memory-reflection as separate, visible components because they correspond directly to this
paper's headline claims (explainability, cold-start robustness via memory), while folding
goal-planning's constraint logic into profiling and recommendation.

\textbf{Profiling \& Constraint Agent.} Extracts a structured student-constraint object
(academic background, career goal, and financial, location, and employability-outcome
constraints) from the student's message for the current session. Extraction is
single-turn per session, not an interactive clarifying-question loop within a session;
continuity across sessions comes from the Memory \& Reflection Agent's persisted state,
which this agent consumes as prior context and updates rather than re-deriving from
scratch (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, dashed arrow).

\textbf{Retrieval Agent.} Selects the programs worth presenting to this student from a
deterministic hard-constraint prefilter's output (admission, budget, and location
feasibility checked against the constraint object; programs failing a hard constraint
are dropped before any agent sees them). This is LLM-based relevance selection over an
in-context candidate list narrowed by the prefilter, not vector or embedding retrieval
against an external knowledge base; the broader retrieval-plus-generation pattern this
stage follows is established in \cite{ragpersonalisedrecommendations}.

\textbf{Recommendation \& Planning Agent.} Ranks retrieved candidates against the
student-constraint object under multiple, potentially conflicting objectives (academic fit,
financial feasibility, location, employability outcome) -- the mechanism RQ2
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) evaluates directly.

\textbf{Memory \& Reflection Agent.} Reviews the proposed shortlist for constraint
violations and weaker-than-available candidates before it reaches the Explanation
Agent, and persists the student-constraint object and shortlist across sessions so
later sessions start from prior state -- the mechanism RQ3 evaluates for
session-over-session adaptivity. A second, deterministic hard-constraint check runs
after this agent as a final guarantee independent of its judgment.

\textbf{Explanation Agent.} Generates a per-recommendation natural-language rationale
grounded in the evidence the Retrieval Agent surfaced, addressing the explainability
limitation \cite{recommendersyshighered} documents in classical systems.

Two deterministic, non-agentic components wrap this five-agent pipeline and are the
mechanism that actually guarantees hard-constraint satisfaction, rather than relying on
agent judgment alone: a prefilter before the Retrieval Agent removes programs violating
the student's admission, budget, or location constraints, and a post-reflection guard
repeats the same check after the Memory \& Reflection Agent, in case a revision
reintroduced a violation. Both are described further alongside the evaluation results
they support (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

This merge (7$\to$5 agents) was a judgment call, argued from buildability within a
single-author prototype's scope rather than derived from an ablation; the H3 results
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) provide the retrospective check. Memory and reflection were
evaluated as a single fused mechanism (the no-memory ablation removes both together),
and that fused mechanism measurably improved session-over-session accuracy and
stabilized recall relative to the stateless pipeline. Nothing in the results motivates
splitting the two responsibilities back apart: the merge did not visibly cost either
the memory or the reflection function room to operate. A finer-grained ablation that
disables memory persistence and shortlist self-critique independently would be needed
to state this more strongly, and is left to future work.

\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Research questions}

\textbf{RQ1.} Does the architecture in Section~\ref{sec:architecture} improve
explainability and adaptivity over classical educational recommenders
(\cite{hrspca,studieme,multidmensionalrecsysuni}) while remaining within a 5\%
non-inferiority margin of their accuracy on standard recommendation metrics
(precision@$k$, recall@$k$, NDCG)?

\textbf{RQ2.} How should multi-objective constraints (academic fit, career goals,
financial constraints, location, employability outcomes) be represented and reasoned over
by specialized agents, compared to a single monolithic LLM prompt or a classical hybrid
scoring function?

\textbf{RQ3.} Do the Memory \& Reflection Agent's mechanisms measurably reduce cold-start
degradation for prospective students with no prior interaction history, compared to a
stateless agent pipeline?

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\textbf{H1.} The proposed architecture achieves higher user-perceived explanation quality
than a classical hybrid baseline re-implemented from \cite{hrspca,studieme}, while
remaining within a pre-registered\footnote{The evaluation design in this section --
metric choice, $k=5$, and the 5\% margin -- was committed to the paper's public
repository (commit \texttt{9b47ce4}, 2026-07-17) before any agentic-condition result
was computed; those results were run and written up in a later commit (\texttt{ba7757e},
2026-07-20). Both commits are visible in the released artifact's git history.} 5\%
\emph{relative} non-inferiority margin on NDCG@$k$ (headline metric; precision@$k$ and
recall@$k$ reported as secondary), on a held-out admissions/preference dataset.

\textbf{H2.} Explicit multi-objective agent reasoning improves recommendation relevance
for students with conflicting constraints (e.g., financial limits vs.\ top academic fit)
relative to a single-objective ranking baseline.

\textbf{H3.} The Memory \& Reflection Agent mitigates cold-start degradation more
effectively than a stateless agent pipeline, measured across simulated first-session vs.\
later-session interactions.

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:planned-methodology}

Evaluation uses a dual-dataset design. The intended primary accuracy benchmark for H1
is the university-rating dataset of \cite{universityrecsyselahi} (1{,}515 ratings, 80
users, 551 universities, plus metadata for 12{,}003 crawled universities) -- real
preference data at exactly this paper's decision scope, available from its authors on
request. That request was still pending when experiments ran, so the results in
Section~\ref{sec:results} use the synthetic benchmark described next, and the
self-constructed-benchmark limitation this implies is stated in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The synthetic constraint-preference dataset is the designed testbed for H2 and H3 --
conflicting-constraint and multi-session cold-start conditions cannot be expressed in
rating-only data, and public educational datasets with such structure are scarce
\cite{slreducationalrecsys} -- and additionally carries H1 while the real dataset
remains unavailable. It is released with the paper's artifact and regenerates
deterministically (fixed seed) from: (i) 300 synthetic students whose academic features
are sampled rows of the public 20{,}000-record IT-student corpus of
\cite{careerrecfeatureselection}, preserving realistic co-occurrence of grades, skills,
and career labels, with financial, location, mobility, and employability-priority
constraints attached by documented sampling rules; (ii) a fictional catalog of 200
programs (invented institutions in real cities, with tuition, placement, salary, and
visa values drawn from realistic country- and tier-calibrated ranges); and (iii) graded
relevance (0--3) for every student--program pair from a published, system-independent
oracle utility function over academic fit, financial feasibility, location fit, and
employability, fixed before any system was evaluated and documented in full in the
released dataset card. Each student carries a three-session progressive-disclosure
script; dataset-wide, 162 of 300 students have an academically best-fitting program
that is financially infeasible (the H2 conflict condition), 61 have deliberately vague
session-1 career goals, and 67 carry a scripted session-3 budget-revision event -- a
stress-test subset released with the dataset for future finer-grained analysis, distinct
from the H3 evaluation cohort described next, which tests general three-session
adaptivity rather than isolating this specific event.

\subsection{Baselines and experimental setup}

Three baselines separate, respectively, the value of agentic methods over classical
educational recommenders and the value of multi-agent decomposition over a monolithic
LLM: (a) a re-implementation of HRSPCA's cascaded knowledge-rule and
historical-predictor pipeline \cite{hrspca}; (b) a re-implementation of StudieMe's
content-based similarity ranker \cite{studieme}; and (c) a single-prompt LLM baseline
that receives the same student profile and candidate evidence as the full pipeline but
performs profiling, ranking, and explanation in a single call, following the ablation
logic of \cite{onlinecoursemaskg}. The classical baselines were re-implemented from the
original papers' full texts rather than approximated with library components; every
underspecified or catalog-adaptation detail was resolved by a logged best-faith choice
(13 choices; appendix reconstruction table), and the two original components that could
not be expressed in the evaluation catalog are omissions that favor the baselines
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). All agentic conditions run on a local open-weights
Gemma model via Ollama (tag \texttt{gemma4:e4b}; a 12B-class variant is available for
an optional model-scale ablation); the released artifact is provider-agnostic (any
local model or bring-your-own API key).

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\textbf{Metrics.} The headline metric is NDCG@5 with graded relevance (0--3, the
oracle's four-level grade; Section~\ref{sec:planned-methodology}), chosen over
precision/recall alone for two reasons: it is rank-sensitive, rewarding a system that
places the best-fitting program first rather than fifth, which flat precision/recall
cannot distinguish; and it consumes the oracle's graded relevance directly, rather than
collapsing it to a binary relevant/not-relevant cutoff and discarding information
about \emph{how well} a program fits. $k=5$ matches both the classical baselines' own
reconstructed output length (Section~\ref{sec:planned-methodology}, appendix) and a
plausible shortlist size for a student to actually review. Precision@5 and recall@5 are
still reported as secondary metrics, since they are the metrics most classical
recommender-system evaluations report \cite{recommendationsys} and so are what a reader
comparing against prior work will look for first, under the pre-registered 5\% relative
non-inferiority margin against the strongest classical baseline (H1). All systems are
evaluated on the same held-out split of 100 students; the remaining 200 students serve
as the historical pool for the HRSPCA predictor stage.

\textbf{Explanation quality} is scored by LLM-as-judge on a three-dimension rubric
(clarity, groundedness in the provided program evidence, usefulness; 1--5 each),
following the protocol direction of \cite{advisingwise}. To avoid self-preference bias
-- same-family judges systematically inflate scores -- the judge is drawn from a
different model family than the experiment model (primary judge: Qwen-family 14B-class;
a Mistral-family second judge provides inter-judge agreement), with system identity
blinded and absolute rather than pairwise scoring to avoid position bias. LLM-as-judge
validity is treated as a limitation in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\textbf{Conditions.} (i) the full five-agent pipeline; (ii) a \emph{no-memory} ablation
(H3); (iii) a \emph{single-objective} ablation collapsing the Recommendation \& Planning
Agent's reasoning to academic fit only (H2); (iv) a \emph{single-prompt} LLM baseline
replacing the five-agent decomposition, following the ablation logic in
\cite{onlinecoursemaskg}; and (v) the two re-implemented classical baselines. H1
comparisons use the full-information state (complete profile, mirroring the filled
application form classical systems assume); H3 uses the three-session
progressive-disclosure structure, with simulated-user feedback (which programs the
student liked or disliked) derived from the oracle's per-student utility ranking,
fixed at dataset-generation time and identical across every compared system --
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses this as a limitation rather than an observed
reaction to any system's actual output.

\textbf{The pre-registered accuracy criterion is not met.} The five-agent pipeline
reaches NDCG@5 $= 0.706$, an 11.0\% relative shortfall against HRSPCA -- outside the
5\% margin -- and the single-prompt baseline ($0.830$) outperforms both, so the
architectural decomposition shows no top-$k$ accuracy gain over a single LLM call on
this benchmark. Both verdicts are reported as committed in the evaluation design.

\textbf{Diagnostics} attribute the shortfall to shortlist \emph{abstention}, not
ranking quality. The five-agent system's Reflection stage trims candidates it judges
inadequate and is instructed never to pad with constraint-violating programs: 70\% of
its shortlists contain fewer than five items (mean length 3.9). NDCG@5 penalizes every
unfilled position against a five-item ideal. On the 30 students where the system did
emit five items, it scores $0.859$; measured at emitted length (rank quality of what
was actually shown), it scores $0.827$ against the single-prompt baseline's $0.830$
(paired bootstrap, $n=100$, 10{,}000 resamples: mean difference $-0.005$, 95\% CI
$[-0.024, +0.014]$, crossing zero -- not a statistically significant difference), and
its precision@5 ($0.904$) matches the single-prompt's $0.912$, both well above the
classical baselines. The accuracy gap is
therefore a list-length effect: the pre-registered metric rewards padding to $k$, and
the agentic design's abstention behavior -- arguably desirable in a high-stakes
decision-support setting -- is penalized by construction. We report this as a finding
about metric design tension, not as a revision of the pre-registered verdict.

\textbf{Post-registration revision (v2).} Prompted by the diagnostics above -- and
reported \emph{alongside} the pre-registered system, never in place of it -- a revised
pruning policy (v2) changes exactly one design decision: the Reflection stage may drop
a candidate only for a hard-constraint violation (demoting, rather than removing,
weaker-but-valid candidates), and the shortlist is restored to $k$ with valid
candidates whenever the candidate pool allows. Retrieval, profiling, ranking
objectives, and all safety guards are unchanged. Because v2 was designed after
observing the H1 results, its numbers cannot claim pre-registered status; they test
whether the abstention diagnosis is causally correct. It is: v2 reaches NDCG@5
$= 0.806$ with mean list length restored to 4.8 (from 3.9), clearing the
pre-registered bar (0.753) and slightly exceeding the strongest classical baseline
(HRSPCA, 0.793), while precision@5 is essentially unchanged (0.898 vs.\ v1's 0.904)
and recall@5 rises (0.156 vs.\ 0.129). Restoring list length does not, however,
change the architectural verdict on accuracy: v2 remains 2.9\% relative below the
single-prompt baseline (0.830) on NDCG@5, so the five-agent decomposition shows no
top-$k$ accuracy advantage over a single LLM call on this benchmark under either
pruning policy.

\subsection{Multi-objective conflict (H2)}

On the 56 evaluation-split students whose academically best-fitting program is
financially infeasible (162/300 dataset-wide), the pre-registered comparison fails on
accuracy: under the v1 pruning policy, the multi-objective pipeline scores NDCG@5
$= 0.666$ against 0.718 for the single-objective ablation. Diagnostics again implicate
list length rather than ranking: conflict cases trigger the Reflection stage's
abstention most often, so the multi-objective condition emits the shortest shortlists
of any condition on precisely this subset (mean 3.70 items vs.\ 4.02 single-objective),
paying the same NDCG@5 penalty documented above. Under the v2 policy the
multi-objective pipeline reaches 0.792 on the conflict subset (mean 4.77 items); a
matched single-objective run under the same v2 policy scores 0.793 (paired bootstrap,
$n=56$, 10{,}000 resamples: mean difference $-0.001$, 95\% CI $[-0.017, +0.014]$,
crossing zero -- not a statistically significant difference; precision@5: 0.855 multi
vs.\ 0.837 single; recall@5: 0.194 vs.\ 0.189). \textbf{H2 is therefore not supported
on oracle-graded accuracy}: explicit
multi-objective reasoning loses to the single-objective ablation under the v1 policy
and ties under the matched v2 policy. The oracle's utility function already encodes
all four objectives, so a single-objective ranker over a constraint-prefiltered
candidate pool recovers most of the multi-objective signal on this benchmark; whether
multi-objective reasoning earns its place in \emph{explanations} -- naming the
financial/academic trade-off the student actually faces -- is assessed under
explanation quality below.

\subsection{Cold start and memory (H3)}

On a fixed 30-student progressive-disclosure cohort (the first 30 students in
evaluation order, spanning all three sessions; only 5 of the 30 carry the session-3
budget-revision event reported dataset-wide in
Section~\ref{sec:planned-methodology} -- the cohort tests general three-session
adaptivity rather than isolating that event), the full and no-memory conditions are
identical at session 1 by construction -- memory is empty on first contact (both NDCG@5
$= 0.583$) -- so the memory mechanism cannot and does not affect the very first
session. Its effect appears across sessions: with memory, session-2 accuracy reaches
NDCG@5 $= 0.776$ vs.\ 0.735 without ($+0.041$), and the gap persists at session 3
(0.748 vs.\ 0.729); recall@5 degrades from session 2 to session 3 without memory
(0.126 $\to$ 0.090) but holds with it (0.118 $\to$ 0.115). The direction consistently
favors memory, but the effect is modest at $n = 30$, and the largest single driver of
improvement in both conditions is progressive disclosure itself (both gain
$0.15$--$0.19$ NDCG@5 from session 1 to 2). H3 is therefore supported in direction --
memory improves session-over-session adaptivity and stabilizes recall -- with the
caveat that the pre-registered phrase ``cold-start mitigation'' is more precisely an
\emph{adaptivity} gain: the first-session cold start is unaffected by construction.

\subsection{Explanation quality (H1)}

Table~\ref{tab:results-explanation} reports cross-judge mean rubric scores (1--5) on
the full 100-student evaluation split for the four conditions that generate
natural-language output, alongside the two classical baselines (which do not) for a
complete comparison against Table~\ref{tab:results-h1}.
\textbf{H1's explanation-quality claim is supported}, and by a wide margin on the two
dimensions that most directly bear on trustworthy decision support: groundedness (4.92
v1 / 4.92 v2 vs.\ 3.96 single-prompt) and usefulness (3.65 / 3.60 vs.\ 2.80). Clarity
shows a smaller but consistent gap (4.11 / 4.06 vs.\ 3.69). The v1/v2 pruning-policy
change that materially affected accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:results}) leaves explanation
quality essentially unchanged, indicating the two properties are largely independent in
this architecture.

\textbf{Isolating architecture from instruction.} The single-prompt baseline's
explanation task is a weaker instruction than \textsc{Explain}'s: it asks for one
``grounded paragraph'' rather than \textsc{Explain}'s explicit directive to trace every
claim to the provided evidence and name the trade-off. This confounds architecture with
instruction wording -- the gap above could be caused by either. A fourth condition,
single-prompt (grounded), holds the single-call architecture fixed and upgrades only its
explanation instruction to \textsc{Explain}'s directive word-for-word (Table~\ref{tab:results-h1}). It scores clarity $3.81$, groundedness
$4.47$, usefulness $3.23$ -- between the ungrounded baseline and the five-agent
conditions on every dimension, closing roughly half of the groundedness and usefulness
gap (53\% and 51\% of the v1 margin, respectively) but only 29\% of the clarity gap,
while its NDCG@5 ($0.831$) and precision@5 ($0.926$) match or slightly exceed the
ungrounded baseline (Table~\ref{tab:results-h1}), so this instruction change costs
nothing on ranking accuracy. Grounding instructions and architectural decomposition
therefore both contribute to the explanation-quality advantage, with neither alone
accounting for it: an explicit grounding instruction closes about half the gap on its
own, and the remaining half -- plus most of the clarity gap -- is attributable to the
architecture (curated, agent-selected evidence and a dedicated explanation step,
isolated from ranking and profiling).

The groundedness gap is corroborated by a direct failure signal both judges were asked
to surface: an explicit list of invented claims -- factual assertions not traceable to
the student's constraints or the retrieved program data -- per explanation. The
single-prompt baseline was flagged for at least one invented claim in 64/100
explanations by the primary judge and 33/100 by the agreement judge (e.g., asserting a
program's tuition or location without that field appearing in the evidence the model
was given); adding the grounding instruction alone roughly halves this, to 42/100 and
6/100 respectively; the five-agent conditions were flagged in 5/100 (v1) and 9/100 (v2)
cases by the primary judge and 0/100 by the agreement judge. The instruction change
closes most of the gap on this specific failure signal but not all of it, mirroring the
partial (53\%) closure on mean groundedness score above -- consistent with an
architecture whose remaining edge comes from a dedicated explanation step reading
agent-curated evidence, not from prompt wording alone.

Inter-judge agreement (mean absolute score difference) is tight for the five-agent
conditions (0.09--0.15 on groundedness, 0.11 on clarity) and markedly looser for the
single-prompt baseline (1.26 on groundedness; 0.89 for the grounded variant) -- the two
independent judges disagree most exactly where the single-prompt-family explanations
are hardest to verify, itself weak evidence for lower groundedness. Returning to H2 (Section~\ref{sec:results}, conflict subset): restricting the same
cross-judge analysis to the 56 financially-constrained students reproduces the gap
essentially unchanged (groundedness 4.96 v1 / 4.91 v2 vs.\ 3.94 single-prompt;
usefulness 3.65 / 3.57 vs.\ 2.80) -- so while multi-objective reasoning does not win on
oracle-graded ranking, it does produce more reliably grounded and useful explanations
for exactly the students facing a real trade-off, which is the qualitative form in
which H2's contribution survives.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The gap argument in Section~\ref{sec:related} was verified against full-text readings of
the decisive prior work: all three agentic-education systems
\cite{advisingwise,agenticedurec,onlinecoursemaskg} were confirmed to address only
already-enrolled students (advising, learning pathways, course selection), and the two
re-implemented classical baselines \cite{hrspca,studieme} were confirmed to contain no
agentic, dialogue, or memory elements. Peripheral Cluster A citations remain verified at
the abstract level only.
The 7-to-5 agent merge (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) is a buildability judgment, not an
empirical result, and may not survive the ablation in H3.

Beyond the gap-verification scope, this work carries the following limitations, stated
before results are in so that none can be read as post-hoc rationalization.

\textbf{Oracle circularity.} Ground-truth relevance in the synthetic dataset is a
published utility function fixed before any system was evaluated, not human judgment. A
system that reverse-engineered the formula would score perfectly. Mitigations: the
oracle is documented in the released dataset card, identical for all compared systems,
and H1's primary benchmark remains the real-preference dataset of
\cite{universityrecsyselahi} if access is granted; explanation quality is judged
independently of the oracle.

\textbf{Synthetic evaluation data.} The program catalog is fictional (real cities,
invented institutions, calibrated but invented economics), student constraints are
attached by documented sampling rules, and the three-session structure is scripted
rather than observed user behavior, with simulated-user feedback derived from the
oracle. Career coverage is IT-only, inherited from the seed corpus of
\cite{careerrecfeatureselection}; generalization beyond IT-bound students is untested.
The scarcity of public educational datasets motivating this choice is itself documented
\cite{slreducationalrecsys}.

\textbf{Baseline reconstruction fidelity.} Both classical baselines were re-implemented
from their papers' full texts, with every underspecified detail resolved by a logged
best-faith choice (appendix reconstruction table). Two original components could not be
expressed in the evaluation catalog (HRSPCA's college-capacity rule; StudieMe's
faculty-matching stage); both omissions remove hard filters and therefore favor the
baselines, not the proposed system.

\textbf{LLM-as-judge validity.} Explanation-quality scores come from LLM judges, not
human raters. Cross-family judging, blinding, absolute scoring, and two-judge agreement
reduce -- but do not eliminate -- known judge biases; this study performs no
human-subject validation of the judge scores, which should be read accordingly.

\textbf{Single experiment model and scale.} All agentic conditions run on one local
open-weights model (\texttt{gemma4:e4b}); conclusions about the architecture are
conditioned on this model class. The evaluation split is $n=100$ students against a
200-program catalog -- larger than the closest classical baselines' user studies
\cite{universityrecsyselahi,bertopicprogramrec} but small in absolute terms.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper identified pre-admission, cross-institution university and program selection as
a decision scope left unaddressed by both classical educational recommenders (pre-agentic
methods) and 2025--2026 agentic education systems (in-program scope only), and proposed
and evaluated a five-agent architecture for this gap. The evaluation's central finding is
a dissociation: the architecture does not demonstrate a top-$k$ accuracy advantage over
either classical baselines or a single-prompt LLM ablation, but it demonstrates a
consistent, judge-corroborated advantage in explanation groundedness and usefulness, and
this advantage persists specifically on the multi-objective conflict cases that motivated
RQ2. An ablation isolating instruction wording from architecture (single-prompt with
\textsc{Explain}'s grounding directive added) shows the explanation-quality advantage is
not purely architectural: an explicit grounding instruction alone closes roughly half of
it, and the remainder is attributable to the architecture's dedicated explanation step
over agent-curated evidence. A memory-and-reflection mechanism modestly improves
adaptivity across sessions relative to a stateless pipeline (RQ3). Together these results
reframe the paper's contribution: the value of agentic multi-agent decomposition in this
decision scope lies in explainable, trustworthy decision support rather than in ranking
accuracy, at least at the model scale and dataset size evaluated here -- and about half of
even that explainability advantage could be recovered from a single prompt with a
better-written instruction, underscoring that the architecture's remaining, narrower edge
comes specifically from separating evidence retrieval from explanation generation. Future work should prioritize three
directions raised directly by the results: human-subject validation of the LLM-as-judge
explanation scores; evaluation against the real preference dataset of
\cite{universityrecsyselahi} should it become available, to remove the self-constructed-
benchmark limitation; and a finer-grained ablation separating memory persistence from
shortlist reflection, which this paper's five-agent design evaluated only as a fused
mechanism. The prototype, evaluation harness, and synthetic dataset are released as an
open-source artifact so these directions -- and independent scrutiny of the results
reported here -- do not depend on access to the original author's environment.
